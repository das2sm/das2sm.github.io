Privileged Occupancy Filtering for Fixed-Vocabulary End-to-End Driving

July 2026  ·  ACSI Laboratory, Feng Chia University  ·  advisor: Dr. Yu-Chen Lin
CARLASparseDriveV2Fail2DrivePyTorchexperimental design
manuscript in preparation

Score-and-select driving planners choose one trajectory from a fixed, learned proposal set. This project asks a narrow question: if you had perfect, privileged knowledge of where every object in the scene is going to be, and the only thing you were allowed to do with that knowledge is reject proposals and re-select from what the planner already produced — would that actually reduce collisions?

Summary

Modern end-to-end planners like SparseDriveV2 generate up to 1,024 candidate trajectories per frame and pick one by a learned score. That scoring is fast, but it reasons through a fixed vocabulary — and objects outside that vocabulary can be effectively invisible to the planner, which is exactly the failure mode that matters most in distribution-shift scenarios.

I built a filter that sits between the planner's proposal set and the controller. It never generates new trajectories and never brakes on its own — it only rejects proposals whose footprint intersects occupancy, and re-selects the highest-scored proposal that's left. I evaluated it against SparseDriveV2 on 185 Fail2Drive routes across 3 seeds, in a design built specifically to survive the confounds that killed my first two attempts at this experiment (more on that below).

Result

Temporal occupancy filtering reduced collision-route incidence by 4.0 percentage points relative to the unmodified planner (95% CI [−7.7, −0.4]), negative at all three seeds, while satisfying a pre-specified route-completion guardrail. Driving Score, Success Rate, and timeout incidence all moved favorably. The direct temporal-vs-current-frame comparison did not resolve at 95% confidence.

Why this design, specifically

I used CARLA's ground-truth actor state as the occupancy source — not a learned occupancy model — on purpose. This is an upper-bound experiment. If privileged, perfect occupancy can't fix a planner's failures by reselecting among its own proposals, the failure isn't a perception problem. It's that the proposal set itself doesn't contain a safe trajectory for that scene. That's a more informative answer than "our occupancy model helped a little," and it isolates exactly one variable: does having a time-correct map of the world let you pick a better trajectory from what you already have?

Three arms

ArmOccupancy sourceSelection rule
Baseline—Unmodified planner (argmax of post-rescore score)
Current-framePresent occupancy, repeated at every future waypointargmax among occupancy-clear proposals
TemporalConstant-velocity propagated occupancy, aligned to each of the planner's 6 waypoint timesargmax among occupancy-clear proposals

The current-frame arm is a control, not a strawman: it isolates whether time alignment specifically matters, or whether any occupancy-based veto would do about the same thing. Freezing the world at the present instant treats every moving car as a stationary wall — which turned out to matter a lot (see below).

Fallback logic

The filter never brakes and never invents a trajectory. If every proposal is occupied, or the occupancy grid is invalid, or nothing valid remains, it keeps the planner's original choice. Every one of five fallback reasons is logged, and driving failures — collisions, timeouts, blockages, incomplete routes — are kept as outcomes, not treated as errors to retry away.

Getting the evaluation right took two failed campaigns

The first version of this study looked great and was wrong, twice, for two different reasons. I'm including this because the final result is only worth trusting because of what got thrown out first.

Attempt 1: a 20-point effect that wasn't real

On a 15-route development split, temporal filtering cut collisions from 46.7% to 26.7% — a 20-point apparent reduction. Fifteen routes isn't enough to estimate anything; the split existed only to confirm the mechanism activated. On the actual 184-route test campaign, the effect nearly vanished: −2.7 points, confidence interval spanning zero. The dev split's headline number didn't survive contact with a real sample.

Attempt 2: a real-looking effect I couldn't attribute

I redesigned the filter to preserve route progress rather than just vetoing hard, and the next campaign showed collisions dropping from 83/185 to 70/185 — thirteen fewer collision routes, in the right direction, on the metric that mattered.

I didn't publish it. Every route in that campaign ran in the same fixed order — baseline, then temporal, then progress-preserving — on a long-lived CARLA process. Treatment and execution position were perfectly correlated, and all six of the campaign's extreme runaway-acceleration episodes landed in whichever arm ran first. I couldn't separate "the filter works" from "running second and third is easier on the simulator," so I couldn't claim the result. I kept the underlying filter implementation and discarded the campaign.

A result you can't attribute to a cause isn't a result.

What the final campaign fixed

  • Three evaluation seeds, not one — CARLA is non-deterministic enough that single-seed campaigns produced different collision counts for the identical baseline and temporal arms.
  • A fresh CARLA server and evaluator process for every single job — no long-lived process, no state carried between arms.
  • All six arm-order permutations balanced across the campaign, so treatment is orthogonal to execution position.
  • Route completion, collision definition, and interpretation criteria for a positive / null / negative / heterogeneous result all written down before any outcome was analyzed.

185 routes × 3 seeds × 3 arms = 1,665 jobs. While reviewing Fail2Drive's own evaluation code for this campaign I also found a scenario-resolution bug that could silently skip test scenarios — reported it, and it's now fixed upstream.

Why current-frame filtering doesn't clearly beat baseline

Frame-level logging shows current-frame occupancy flags the planner's original choice on 54.6% of frames, but only 22.4% of those flagged frames actually have a clear alternative to switch to — the rest get rejected outright with no safe proposal left. Freezing the world at the present instant makes every moving actor a permanent obstacle, which is overly restrictive rather than more protective. Temporal filtering shows the same pattern less severely (48.1% flagged, 21.1% with a clear alternative), which is consistent with why it outperforms the frozen-world control, even though that specific pairwise comparison didn't reach significance on its own.

Limitations

  • Occupancy comes from privileged CARLA simulator state, not a learned perception model — this isolates whether reselection helps at all, given perfect information, and says nothing about a deployable perception-driven version.
  • The occupancy grid is 2D with a fixed vertical slab. Overhead structures (bridges, gantries) can be flagged identically to ground-level obstacles.
  • Dynamic actors are propagated at constant velocity with fixed yaw — no acceleration, intent, or interaction modeling.
  • One planner, one benchmark, 185 routes, three seeds. No generalization claim beyond SparseDriveV2 on Fail2Drive.
  • The filter only rejects and reselects. It has no braking authority and cannot generate a trajectory outside the planner's original 1,024 proposals.
